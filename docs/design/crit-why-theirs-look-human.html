Design crit · Tasks website 10 human sites vs 7 of our designs · 12 Nov 2026

They spend on one real thing. We spend on everything else.

Each human site has one thing no one else could ship: a made image, or real live data. They keep everything around it boring. Our designs have neither, and we restyle every button, nav and footer to look clever. That is the gap.

Human: Cofounder, Mercury, Kitchen, Zapier, Linear, Tailscale, Ramp, Figma, JetBrains, Coinbase. Ours: the shipped Grid app, the three earlier prototypes (Week desk, Night studio, Day line) and my three new directions (A Telegram, B Rapid log, C Two-pass print). I judged only what the screenshots show. I could not see motion.

Eight findings, worst first

Ranked by how much each one makes our work read as machine-made.

1

They pay for one real thing. We pay for nothing real.

Them

A photo shoot of a desk on a misty ridge. A whole pixel-art world. One ink character who appears in every drawing. A dithered film still of a control tower. A glossy 3D fan rendered in the brand colors. Each took a person days, and no other company can ship it.

Us

Seven designs, zero images, zero real data. Everything is type, 1px rules and CSS boxes. Even my "tape strips" in Direction A are CSS. Day line uses colored icon tiles from an icon set, which is the opposite of a made asset.

The exception that proves it: Coinbase

Coinbase has no made art at all. It even uses the split hero (text left, product right) that our rules call a template. It still reads as human, because the product shows real, live data in the visitor's own currency and number format. Your screenshot shows a balance of ₹1,30,535.80, with Indian lakh grouping, not the Western style. The page knows where the visitor is. Our demo data knows nothing about anyone.

FixChoose one real thing before any layout: a made asset (image generation, a photo, a drawing) or real data in the visitor's own locale. Ideally both.

2

Their strangeness lives in one place. Ours is everywhere.

Them

On all ten sites the nav is the same dull row: logo, a few category words, Log in, one strong button. The footers are plain link columns. The buttons are plain pills. Only the hero art or the hero data is special.

Us

Grid puts the nav inside a red poster field. Direction A turns the nav into a "printed band", the FAQ into "conditions on the back" and the footer into a "receipt stub". That is a costume, and a costume is what a model makes when it is told to be distinctive.

FixKeep nav, buttons, forms and footer conventional. Allow one strange moment on the whole page.

3

They show the product. We show a metaphor about the product.

Them

Figma's hero is a stack of real work made in Figma. JetBrains shows a real IDE with an autocomplete popup open. Coinbase sets its live price list at headline size. Even the metaphor sites (Mercury, JetBrains Air) use the metaphor for one image, then show the product.

Us

All three new directions open with a telegram, a notebook or a print shop. The real app does not appear until section 4. A visitor who leaves after one screen knows our metaphor but not our app.

FixPut the real app, big and cut off by the edge, in the first screen.

4

Their proof has names, dates and sources. Ours has engineer facts.

Them

A named design director with a photo, beside a percentage with its data month. A developer count set larger than the sentence around it. Case-study links under every number. Exact fee and order-type names instead of adjectives.

Us

We have no users to quote, and we invented none. That is right. But my directions filled the gap with a test count and "contrast checked by script". A visitor does not care how many unit tests pass. That proof is for us, not for them.

FixGet 3 to 5 real people to use it for a week and quote them, with permission. Until then, demonstrate (the live quick-add) and claim nothing.

5

Our four "different" designs live the same invented life.

Them

Demo data points at a real world: a bird-song recording with a timestamp and a town in Ontario, a chat thread with first names and a real time, a receipt for an exact amount at a named store, a balance in the visitor's own currency.

Us

The same four tasks appear in all four of our app designs. Everybody's AI to-do list has a landlord, a boiler and a fig tree. Change the colors and it is still one dataset, which tells the viewer that one author made all of it.

6

They make one dumb, huge move. We stack clever small ones.

Them

Figma ends the page with one short line and one black button the full width of the screen. JetBrains sets one number twice the size of its sentence. Coinbase sets coin names at headline size. No explanation, no second idea.

Us

Grid has a poster numeral, a rolling animation, a red strike, ink rules and a "when" rail. Direction A, after my process added its "raises", has tape, a stamp, a word counter, "COST: NONE", four stations, a night letter, local time and source captions. Each is clever. Together they are noise.

FixDelete ideas until one is left. Make that one bigger than feels safe.

7

Their surfaces have a hand. Ours are perfect.

Them

Pixel steps in the type, dither noise in the photo, wobble in the ink line, grain in the color panels, a real photo of a bird. Six of ten sites carry visible texture or a human hand.

Us

Perfect circles, exact hairlines, flat fills, every spacing value on the scale. Nothing in any of our designs could only have been made by a person.

FixThe made asset from finding 1 carries the texture. The UI around it stays clean.

8

They use "slop" when it is theirs. Our rules make us dodge it.

Them

JetBrains ships a purple-to-magenta glossy 3D render, four equal product cards and generic lines about empowering your team. Coinbase ships the split hero. Our slop list bans all of it. It works for them because the colors, the render and the data are their own.

Us

Our ban lists push us to clever avoidance: no cards, so poster rules; no gradients, so overprint; no plain hero, so a telegram. Avoiding the category is itself predictable, and it is its own machine look.

FixChange the test from "is this element banned?" to "could any other company ship this exact thing?"

Where we already win

Ruthless both ways. The human sites are not better at everything.

Product craft

Our app screens have real states, keyboard hints, overdue logic and a detail view. Most reference pages only show product pieces.

Honesty

We invented no customers, counts or quotes. Several reference pages lean on numbers a visitor cannot check.

Their copy is not magic

Some human headlines are generic, about friction, productivity and success. They win on real things and proof, not on words.

New rules for the next round

These replace the parts of my three directions that fail the tests above.

  1. Real app first. The first screen shows the real app, large, cut off by the edge, with the live quick-add working.
  2. One real thing. A made asset, or real data in the visitor's locale. Choose it before layout. It carries all the strangeness.
  3. Boring chrome. Nav, buttons, forms and footer follow convention.
  4. One move. One oversized, simple move per page. Everything else serves it.
  5. Real data, real people. Demo data from a real week. Quotes only from real users, with permission. No engineer facts as proof.
  6. The new slop test. Could another company ship this exact thing? If yes, it is slop, banned or not.

Three things only you can give

  1. The made asset's source: AI image generation, a photo you take, or a drawing.
  2. Your real week of tasks for the demo data. I can change names.
  3. 3 to 5 people who will try the app and give a one-line quote.
